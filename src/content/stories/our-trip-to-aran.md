My brother Shai felt the need to connect with our paternal grandfather, Abraham Simha (Sam, or Simkhl) Kubitsky, who died in Boston when Chuck was three years old. I was seven, and have some faint memories of Grandpa. Our younger sister, Tamar Simha, is named after him.

So Shai decided to pay a roots visit to Grandpa’s birthplace in Lithuania: Aran by its Jewish name, or Varėna in Lithuanian. Look for Senoji Varėna, or Old Varėna; Varėna was reestablished at a new location ca. 1895, in order to get closer to the railroad that the Russians had built.

Shai invited me to come along. We flew a low-cost to Vilnius (Vilna, in Yiddish), and enlisted a guide for the day, Daniel Gurevich, a local Jew who speaks good Hebrew, English, Russian and Lithuanian, and knows his trade. We spent the two following days recuperating as pretend-tourists in Vilnius.

Varėna is an hour drive south-west of Vilnius. Today Jewish Aran is contained in its entirety in the old cemetery, within a fenced perimeter of primal forest. The plaque at the entrance was done by a visual artist, who slipped in a spelling error that a Hebrew-speaker wouldn’t make.

The humidity and overgrowth produces swarms of gnats and other winged beasts. As we visit, we come across an intrepid work crew cutting away at the overgrowth. The town is required to maintain the cemetery; otherwise, it would become totally overgrown in a matter of months. Take a look at Google Street View.

We go through the cemetery, examining the Hebrew lettering that is still decipherable. About two dozen headstones stand erect with readable inscriptions; others are knocked over, and yet others lie in shallow graves of their own, covered with earth and plant growth. We find no Kubitkys here, however – but read on.

After saying Kadish we stroll the streets of Old Varėna, passing by abandoned houses that could be a hundred years old, and others that look well maintained, windows veiled with white lace curtains, large manicured yards boasting flowers in full bloom and beds of vegetables for home use. Outside the cemetery, a stork-nest with young is perched atop a telephone pole. You can see it, empty, in Street View.

![Walking through the streets of Old Varėna](/Chanans-literary-site/images/our-trip-to-aran/old-varena-road.jpg)

We could relate to the epithet that the Vilner Jews awarded the Araners: *di Araner meisim* (די אראנער מייתים) – the Aran dead – for leading such a quiet, uneventful, ho-hum existence.

Danny explains that the Lithuanian is typically introverted in nature, a slow but deep thinker, who could also be quite stubborn. Add to that a unique, subtle, refined sense of humor and you get a perfect characterization of our father and, perhaps, his. Well, maybe not always refined.

We go to the stream that flows through Varėna, the Merkys River. The water is very clear, showing a strong, steady flow, water plants swaying with the current. I am reminded of the times Grandpa took me to Norumbega pond to feed the ducks’ pipicks with breadcrumbs. Alas, there are neither ducks nor pipicks in the Merkys.

![On the bridge over the Merkys River in Varėna](/Chanans-literary-site/images/our-trip-to-aran/merkys-bridge.jpg)

After we have our fill with Aran, its cemetery, storks, stream, flowers and vegetable gardens, Danny takes us to the site, a couple of kilometers away, at a clearing in the midst of the forest – of a mass grave of Aran Jews. The plaque in Yiddish and Lithuanian commemorates 360 Jews of Aran, men, women and children, who were killed by the Nazis and their collaborators on September 10th, 1941. All told, 831 Jews of Aran were put to death in the space of a couple of months.

![Mass grave memorial outside Aran for 360 Jews murdered in 1941](/Chanans-literary-site/images/our-trip-to-aran/mass-grave.jpg)

Grandpa Simkhl, his parents, and seven younger siblings escaped that fate; they were long living in the *goldene medina* (the “golden land”). They had landed in the port of Boston on June 4th, 1905 – 109 years to the day before we visited the Araner meisim. So no Kubitskys here – but read on.

–––

IT’S mid-afternoon and we have some time on our hands. We ask Danny if there is anything interesting to see on the way back to Vilnius. Sure, he says, there is a lovely place called Trakai, a resort town with a unique Jewish story. So we drive back about 45 minutes to Trakai. We get a light lunch consisting of a local specialty bun with a meat filling, and a cup of borscht.

Then Danny takes us to the local Jewish cemetery (a typical Lithuanian pastime? –we start to wonder). We enter a neglected patch of land through a rusted gate, stumble over strewn tree branches and boulders, gaze over random Hebrew lettering – and Holy cow!, says Shai, Here is a Kubitsky who managed to get himself buried here!

The Hebrew spelling is funny, but it clearly names Simha Kobetski (שמחה קובעצכי). This is someone else’s Grandpa Simkhl!

![Hebrew inscription on the Simha Kobetski tombstone in Trakai](/Chanans-literary-site/images/our-trip-to-aran/simha-kobetski.jpg)

Then, right by the grave, we find a 3-sided tombstone, in old Cyrillic Russian letters, commemorating a family of 3 Kobetskis. It looks like Joseph and Rachel were a couple, and Esther their daughter.

So we find these tombs, indeed stumble over them, but the story gets better. Read on…

It turns out that the Jewish community of Trakai is no run-of-the-mill Jewish Orthodox community. They belong to the Karaite sect that split off rabbinical Judaism in Mesopotamia in the 9th century. The Karaites, whose name derives from the word meaning “scripture”, do not accept the method of *pilpul* that is so central to rabbinical teaching, and thus don’t accept the Talmud. Rather than building upon previous generations’ commentary on Biblical laws, each individual is expected to learn Torah and interpret its laws himself. Karaism makes a fascinating story, well worth reading up on.

We note that the Karaites split into two diasporas: one remained in the Muslim world, centered in Cairo, and the other migrated through Crimea to parts of central Europe. The European Karaites of the 19-20 centuries did their best to distance themselves from the Jews, in order to spare themselves the persecution that was so often their lot in life. This worked fairly well during the Shoah, for example, when in Lithuania they survived the rounding up of the Jews.

Today the Karaites have a small presence in Trakai. There is a Karaite street and a schul of distinctive design called Knesa. That specialty pastry we had for lunch, *kibinai*, is attributed to Karaite cuisine and is unique to Trakai. However, living members of the sect would much rather be considered Christians of Turkmen-Persian descent than Jews.

–––

FAMILY tradition has it that we were originally called Dubrow; Kubitsky was a name acquired by an ancestor, perhaps Grandpa Simkhl’s father Kalman or his father. The “Cantonist Law”, in force during the second quarter of the 19th century, allowed the Russian army to conscript young Jewish boys for up to 31 years of military service. By that time they were lost to their families and lost to Judaism. An only child, or perhaps even a first-born, was exempt. So the Jews got into mixing and matching their children with phony adoptions and assumed names so as to appear as only children.

–––

THE QUESTION remains: how are we connected with the Karaite Kobetskis? Are we Karaites ourselves?

My scientific training leads me to seek the simpler explanation. This may be a letdown, but here goes: Papa Dubrow has business in Vilnius. So he puts a couple of kopeks together and books a ride with the horse-drawn coach. They stop on the way for refreshment in Trakai, where he is served by a Kobetsky. Papa Dubrow likes the name, particularly because it is relatively immune as a Karaite; he gives the name to one of his boys who are in danger of conscription under the Cantonist Law. No need for adoption, just bribe the town registrar with the other kopek he had saved.

There is the possibility that the Karaite connection was a bit stronger. Grandpa Abraham Simha Kubitsky was born in 1881; the Karaite Simha Kobetski probably died about then – the other Kobetskis, whose tombs we found nearby, all died in the 1880s. Grandpa Simkhl may have been named in honor of Simha, indicating a closer than casual relationship.
